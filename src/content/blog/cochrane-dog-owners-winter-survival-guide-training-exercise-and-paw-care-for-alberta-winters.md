Winters in Cochrane, like anywhere else in Alberta, affect how you live with your dog. One week, Chinooks made a midday walk easy. Then the temperature drops, sidewalks are frozen, and a longer walk didn’t feel good to either you or your dog. Good [dog training in Cochrane](/) helps you through all of it.

Regardless of whether it’s raining or the sun is shining, your dog needs regular opportunities to exercise, follow routines, spend energy, and have meaningful things to do. Additionally, protecting your dog’s paws from the ice, snow, salt and chemicals used on our streets and sidewalks during the winter months is important.

Fortunately, getting your dog active during winter doesn’t require a long walk every day. A combination of short trips outdoors, indoor training, activities, exercise, and a structured daycare provides your dog with an opportunity to remain active regardless of the weather.

## When It Is Too Cold for a Long Walk, Train Indoors

A dog does not need an hour outside to have a productive day, especially if the temperature falls towards -30 degrees Celsius. A five- to ten-minute training session inside will likely be more effective than attempting to extend a walk that neither you nor your dog finds enjoyable.

Both mental and physical activities can be considered “work” for a dog. As such, asking your dog to listen to you (e.g., “stay,” “wait,” etc.) or stay focused (e.g., enjoying a bully stick) can provide them with positive ways to spend their time.

Take advantage of cold winter days to practice basic skills that will help make daily life easier:

- Practice sending your dog to their place or bed and tell them to stay there until released.
- Practice recalling your dog from different rooms in your home.
- Practice basic obedience behaviours such as sitting, lying down, staying, and paying attention to you despite typical household distractions.
- Create treasure hunts using hidden treats or pieces of food.
- Practice calmly walking your dog on a leash through a hallway or another open indoor space.
- Instead of placing their favourite toys directly on the floor, incorporate them into short training exercises and games.

Keep the training sessions relatively short. Five focused minutes several times during the day is easier for most dogs than one long lesson.

Consistency is key here. If your dog typically gets plenty of outdoor time and then suddenly spends several days confined indoors, undesirable behaviour patterns can rapidly develop. Examples include barking, jumping, chewing, pulling, and restlessness, which are usually more challenging to address once they’ve developed due to the lack of a clear outlet. Training redirects the excess of energy appropriately.

## Winter Exercise Doesn’t Have to Stop

Cochrane offers many locations to walk and exercise with your dog when conditions are favourable. The Historic Cochrane Ranche, for example, is a good option for a change of scenery and is connected to Glenbow through a pathway system and leads toward the Bow River. This trail allows you to vary the duration of your walk depending on the current weather conditions.

The Bow River corridor also has numerous options for local dog owners. Riverfront Park features walking paths and a designated off-leash area for dogs. In addition, Jim Uffelmann Memorial Park includes trails adjacent to the Bow River and an off-leash area too.

Of course, winter affects how you utilize these locations.

After snow, watch for packed ice and uneven sections. Conditions on unpaved trails will differ from cleared asphalt paths. Keep your dog leashed unless you are in a clearly marked off-leash area.

Most importantly, watch your dog. If they start lifting their paws, shivering, slowing down, or refusing to continue, they’re telling you something. Head home. There is no benefit in finishing a planned route just because you normally walk that distance.

A ten-minute walk on a suitable winter day is better than pushing through an hour in conditions your dog can’t handle comfortably.

## Booties or Paw Balm: What Works Better?

Snow itself is only part of the issue. Cochrane sidewalks create another winter problem, since ice, road salt, and deicing products can irritate sensitive paw pads.

Dog booties provide the strongest physical barrier between your dog’s feet and the ground and are especially useful when you regularly walk on treated sidewalks or during very cold weather (and very hot weather too). The problem is that some dogs strongly dislike them at first.

Don’t put on four boots for the first time and immediately expect a normal walk. Introduce them indoors, let your dog wear one briefly, reward calm behaviour, and slowly build from there. Walking strangely for the first few minutes is common while a dog gets used to them.

Paw balm or paw wax can also work well for shorter walks and for dogs that refuse to wear boots. Apply it before going outside to create a protective layer between the paw and winter surfaces.

Whichever option you choose, check your dog’s feet during the walk and when you come home. Wipe the paws and between the toes to remove salt, ice, dirt, and any type of debris. Dry them well, look for redness, cracks, cuts, or areas your dog keeps licking.

Paw care takes a minute, while treating a painful or injured paw takes much longer and costs more.

## Adjust Exercise to Your Dog, Not Just the Forecast

A large dog with a thick coat handles cold differently from a small, short-haired dog. This is why there is no single winter walking rule that fits every dog. Puppies and senior dogs also need extra care.

Wind, wet fur, snow depth, and the amount of time spent outside all change how quickly a dog becomes uncomfortable. Watching your dog is more useful than following a fixed walking schedule.

On milder days, take advantage of the weather and enjoy a longer outing. When a Chinook arrives, it gives you a welcome chance to get outside, although melting snow followed by freezing temperatures can leave slippery surfaces behind.

During a deep cold snap, shorten the outdoor portion of the day and move more activity indoors. Keep in mind that winter exercise should be flexible.

## Don’t Put Training on Hold Until Spring

You shorten walks because it is cold. Training happens less often. Your dog gets more restless indoors. Winter can quietly undo good habits and, by February, pulling on the leash or ignoring commands has become normal again.

That’s why dog training in Cochrane should continue through winter. The only thing that changes is where and how you practise:

- Use your living room when it is too cold outside.
- Practise attention during a short walk instead of measuring success by distance
- Use comfortable winter days for longer outings around Cochrane
- Add daycare when your schedule or the weather leaves your dog without enough activity.

Small amounts of consistent work are far more useful than waiting for perfect conditions.

## Build a Winter Routine You Can Actually Keep

You don’t need an elaborate winter schedule.

On a good day, go for a proper walk. On an icy morning, shorten it and add indoor training later. Protect your dog’s paws when sidewalks are salty. Use daycare when several days of bad weather make normal exercise difficult. More importantly, pay attention to your dog.

When you keep training, exercise, paw care, and rest in balance, winter becomes much easier to manage. Your dog stays engaged, you keep building useful habits, and neither of you has to wait for spring to enjoy life together.
